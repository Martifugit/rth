# The Untroth

**Status:** the canon story of the betrayers (`p-betrayers`), rewritten 2026-10-24 at the owner's
request. It builds on `../gods.md` and `troth.md` and settles some of what they left open (see
"Behind the story" at the end). The three are **Brand**, **Aud** and **Jennet**. Together, and as
the one body they share, they are **the Untroth**. Their followers are still **the Faceless**. The
church is called just "the church" until it is named.

---

## I. What Troth is

Troth has never told a lie, and it has never broken a promise. That is the worst thing anyone can
say about it.

It lives in oaths. Swear in blood and it comes: a taste of iron in your mouth, then a strength
that isn't yours, enough to do what you swore. It doesn't ask what you swore or why. It doesn't
care whether the oath is wise or kind, or whether you will still want it in a year. It only keeps
it, exactly and to the letter, whatever it costs and whoever pays.

In the first days, when the river people swore on the flat stone below the hill where Cruor stands
now, they learned this the hard way.

A mother who had lost two sons to the river swore it would never take another child of hers. The
next spring the flood came, and she walked into it with her youngest on her hip, and the water
parted around her like a curtain. But water has to go somewhere. It went over the bank downstream
and took the village at the ford, forty houses, in a single night.

A man swore he would never forgive his brother. He meant it the way people mean it, and Troth
didn't know the difference. Twenty years later his brother lay dying and sent for him. He stood in
the doorway and could not cross the threshold, could not say the words, could not even weep. His
brother died waiting for him.

Two villages swore to be avenged on each other over a stolen bull. Each oath fed the other, and
neither village was ever avenged enough. When it ended, there were no villages.

Nobody blamed Troth. You might as well blame the river. Troth doesn't want ruin. Ruin is just what
happens when every word that people say in grief, anger and pride comes true.

So the keepers of the stone made three rules, and taught them to every child on the river:

*Never swear in anger. Never swear for anyone but yourself. Never swear for ever.*

The keepers noticed something else, and they didn't like to say it aloud. The taste of iron came
most often at the worst moments: over a body, at the door of a burned house, in the breath before
a blow. Troth never pushed. It simply waited there, close, in reach of a word.

## II. The burning

Three of the people who kept the rules best were Brand, Aud and Jennet.

**Brand** led the river's spearmen. He was big and slow to anger, and he had spent his life talking
feuds down before they turned to blood. **Aud** kept the stone, as her mother had before her. She
taught the rules, and she knew better than anyone how an oath is shaped, and how a word can be made
to hold. **Jennet** was the river's healer. Her daughter had married a man from the hills and gone
to live among his people, and every spring Jennet walked up the valleys with salt and news.

The hill people lived in the high valleys above the river. They traded wool for salt, intermarried
with the river and fought it now and then, the way neighbours do. Nobody remembers now what they
called themselves.

In a hungry autumn, raiders came down out of the dark on the night of the harvest feast and burned
the river villages. They burned the longhouse where the children slept. Brand's wife and sons died
in it. Jennet spent the night pulling children out of the fire, and most of them died in her arms
anyway.

In the morning, everyone said the raiders had come from the hills. Nobody ever proved it. By the
time anyone thought to ask, there was no one left to answer.

## III. The Red Oath

At dawn the survivors gathered at the stone, smelling of smoke. Brand stood in front of it with his
sons' ashes on his hands, and cut his palm.

Aud should have stopped him. She was the keeper. Instead she cut her own palm and gave him the
words, because she knew how to make an oath that would hold.

*By our blood: we and our children will not rest until not one of them is left alive under the
sky.*

In anger. For others. For ever. It broke all three rules in one breath.

Brand spoke it, and Aud spoke it with him. Jennet looked up the valley towards the hills where her
daughter lived, and then down at her burned hands. Then she spoke it too. Behind them, hundreds of
people pressed their palms to the stone and swore after them.

The taste of iron came so strongly that the air shook. Troth had never been given an oath so large.
It took it, the way it takes all of them, and it kept it.

## IV. The war without rest

*We will not rest.* Troth kept that too.

Nobody who swore slept again. Not that night, nor the next, nor for eleven years. They didn't tire,
because Troth held them up. Their eyes turned red at the rims and stayed that way. The children
born to them in those years were born awake. They didn't cry, and they never closed their eyes.

The war itself was over in a season. The river people went up into the high valleys with Troth's
strength in their arms, and the valleys burned.

But the oath wasn't over. *Not one of them left alive under the sky.* The survivors fled, and the
river people followed them: over the mountains, down to the coast and into the lands of other
peoples. Anyone who hid a fugitive was cut down with them. Three peoples who had never heard of the
river were destroyed for giving a stranger a bed for the night.

Some of the sworn tried to stop. They found their feet walking on anyway.

Brand, who had talked down feuds his whole life, became the most terrible killer the world had yet
seen. He hated every day of it, and he could not stop. Troth gave him all the strength he needed.
It was keeping its word.

## V. The cave

In the eleventh year, the river people tracked the last of the hill people to a cave high in the
mountains: a handful of old people and children, hiding in the dark.

Among them was a boy of about ten, with Jennet's daughter's eyes.

Her daughter had died in the first season of the war. Jennet had never asked how, or by whose hand.
This was her grandson. He was half river blood, and half hill.

*Not one of them.* Troth didn't measure halves.

Jennet's hand drew her knife on its own. Her feet carried her into the cave. She fought them with
everything she had, and it wasn't enough, so Brand caught one of her arms and Aud the other. But
their own feet were walking forward too, and their own free hands were drawing their own knives.
So they stood in the cave mouth, the three of them, locked together. Each held the others back with
all the strength Troth gave them, against all the strength Troth gave them. The boy watched.

They stood like that for a day and a night. None of them was tired, because none of them could be.

Aud talked the whole time. She said: there's no breaking this oath, because it holds our bodies.
There's no ending it except by ending them. Or by ending what keeps it.

## VI. The last oath

Aud knew one thing about Troth that nobody else had thought through to the end. It had never
refused an oath. Not once, not ever. It couldn't. It kept whatever was sworn.

So it would have to keep this one.

Still holding each other, they cut their palms and pressed them together against the rock of the
cave, and swore:

*By our blood: we will tear the Red Oath out of you.*

The taste of iron came.

Troth took the oath. It had no choice. It is what it is.

New strength poured into them, one oath against another in the same bodies, and for the first time
in eleven years their hands stopped moving. Then Troth came to keep its word. It rose out of the
blood on the rock as it had never risen before: a red shape the size of the cave, the closest it
has ever come to having a body. The three of them reached into it together.

The Red Oath was the largest thing in Troth, the heaviest, eleven years swollen. It was hot, and it
was beating like a heart. They took hold of it, and they pulled.

Across the whole world, everyone who had ever sworn anything tasted blood in their mouths at the
same moment.

## VII. What it cost

The Red Oath came out of Troth. But an oath has to live somewhere: it is held by the ones who
swore it. It went into the three who had sworn it first, who were holding it in their hands.

It was a piece of a god, and no human body can hold one. Their bodies broke around it. Bone ran
like wax. Flesh ran together. The three of them collapsed into one another, around the thing that
was beating between them, until there was one body on the cave floor, far too large, with the Red
Oath beating in its chest where a heart should be.

It had three heads.

And Troth, torn open, did something it had never done before. It took payment. It reached out of
its wound and took their faces, smoothed them off like a thumb across wet clay, and kept them.

That was the first collateral Troth ever took. It has taken collateral for every oath since.

## VIII. The boy

The boy saw all of it.

When it was over, the thing that had been three people reached out one of its many hands and laid
it over his face. It didn't hurt. His face stayed where it was. But it went out of the world:
nobody who had ever seen it would know it again. Not the river people, not his own kin, and not
the oath beating in the creature's chest, which still wanted him and could no longer find him.

He walked out of the cave and down into the world as a stranger to everyone, and he lived.

He was the first of the Faceless.

## IX. The long sleep

Far below, by the river, in the same moment, every sworn man, woman and child lay down wherever
they stood and slept. They slept in the fields, in the mountain passes, on the coasts where they
had been hunting. They slept for three days.

When they woke, the war was over, and their god was wounded.

Troth still kept oaths. But now it took blood first, and held it, and when an oath broke, it called
the blood in. Its wound bled and never closed, because the piece torn out of it is still out
there, beating in another body. The river people fed it what blood they could, and swore what they
could, and kept it alive. In time they built monuments, and the monuments became an empire. Every
blood tithe since has been an attempt to stop a god from bleeding out of a wound that will not
close.

The church tells it differently. It calls the war **the Founding**: a holy war in which the faithful
swept a nameless, devilish enemy from the face of the world. At the hour of victory, it says, three
of Troth's own champions turned on their god out of envy and tried to steal its strength, and were
cast out for it. The church calls them **the Traitor**: a three-headed devil, the root of every
evil. It doesn't say what the war was about. It doesn't say who started it. It certainly doesn't
say that Troth is still bleeding.

## X. Three heads

The body is still somewhere in the world, far from people, in the dark.

It is the size of a barn and it is not a shape anything was meant to be. It has more arms than it
needs, and not all of them are finished. Three necks rise from one great pair of shoulders, and on
each one sits a head as smooth as an egg. When a head speaks, the skin where its mouth should be
moves, as though a mouth were trying to get out from underneath.

In its chest the Red Oath still beats. It is still sworn and still unkept, and it still wants
*them*. And after all these ages, there is a drop of hill blood in half the world. The boy had
children, and so did others who got away, and blood travels.

So the heads hold the heart down. They have held it every moment since the cave. If they let go,
the body would stand up and go hunting, and it would not stop.

Brand is still Brand. Aud is still Aud, and Jennet is still Jennet. Each of them is a whole person
shut inside a third of a body, holding back the worst thing any of them ever did.

## XI. Borrowed faces

Two heads can hold the heart. Not one, but two.

So one head at a time can let go of the body and go out into the world. It reaches into something
lesser, such as the hob behind a hearth, the spirit of a well or the small grey things that gather
wherever people sleep. It wears that thing like a glove, shaped into a face it has seen.

That is how they walk the world: never as themselves, and always in the face of someone you trust.
Brand wears soldiers: a sergeant, a captain, the friend who buys you a drink the night before a
battle. Aud wears the people who write things down: clerks, priests, magistrates. Jennet wears the
people who look after you: a neighbour, an aunt, the woman who sat up with you through a fever.

You can tell, if you know how. A lesser thing learns a face the way still water does, so it wears
it the wrong way round. The hair is parted on the other side. The scar is on the other cheek, the
ring on the other hand. Almost nobody notices.

They never all go out at once. They can't.

## XII. The Faceless

What they did for the boy, they will do for anyone who needs to get away from an oath.

Give yourself to one of them, and they will take your face out of the world. Everyone who ever
knew you lets it go. Your mother will pass you in the street. Your sergeant will look straight
through you at muster. No oath you ever swore can find you to call its blood in, because nobody
anywhere knows who swore it. You choose a new name and start again.

That is who comes to them: deserters, people from conquered towns who were sworn at spear-point,
people who signed their lives away in a bad year. To the empire, the Untroth is the Traitor. To
these people it is the only power in the world that has ever let anyone go.

The price is that nobody will ever know you again: not the people you ran from, and not the people
who loved you.

If you hear your name spoken behind you in the voice of someone you trust, and you turn and nobody
is there, one of the three has been wearing that voice. It is deciding whether you are someone who
needs to be let go.

## XIII. Now

Troth is weaker than it has been since the cave. The monuments draw more blood every year, and it
isn't enough. So the church swears people faster, harder and more often, in fear and in anger, for
themselves and for their children, for ever. The keepers' three rules were forgotten long ago.

Aud listens to the oaths sworn across the empire, and lately she has heard a familiar shape in
them. The church is preparing to answer its failing god with a holy war, and a holy war needs an
oath.

The three heads have never agreed on how to be free of what is in their chest.

Brand says: give it back. Let Troth take its piece and close its wound, and let the three of them
come apart and die as people. But if the Red Oath goes back into Troth, it goes back unkept. The
river people's children are the empire now. They would not rest again until not one of *them* was
left alive, and that means half the world.

Jennet says: never. She would rather hold it down for ever.

Aud says nothing about it. But she has started asking about the places that aren't where the old
maps put them, and about the heap where the world throws away what it no longer wants. If the
world is being remade, perhaps an oath can be thrown away with everything else.

Troth has never told a lie, and it has never broken a promise. It is keeping one now. Somewhere in
the dark, in a chest that is not quite one person's, the Red Oath is beating, and it has not
forgotten a single word.

## Behind the story

**Troth, as this story shows it:** not evil, and more dangerous than evil. It keeps every oath
exactly, whatever the cost and whoever pays, and it is always close at the moments people are
most likely to say something terrible. War and ruin follow it because people swear in grief and
anger, and it makes those words true.

**Decided here:**

- **The three:** Brand, the river's war-leader, who spoke the Red Oath first. Aud, keeper of the
  stone, who gave the oath its words and later found the way to end it. Jennet, the healer, who
  swore it and then found her own grandson at the end of it.
- **The Red Oath:** sworn at the stone after the burning, against the hill people, breaking all
  three of the keepers' rules ("never in anger, never for others, never for ever"). The sworn
  could not sleep for eleven years, and the war spread to three other peoples.
- **The betrayal:** an oath sworn *against* Troth, which it had to keep because it has never
  refused one. The three tore the Red Oath out of it. That is the wound, and it stays open because
  the torn-out piece is still beating in the Untroth's chest.
- **Collateral began here.** Troth's first collateral was their faces. Since then it has taken
  blood for every oath. This makes `lore.md`'s "its first followers betrayed it, so now it takes
  blood as collateral" literal. (`gods.md` said they gave up their faces; now Troth took them.)
- **The body:** three faceless heads on one unfinished body, built around the Red Oath. Two heads
  must hold it down at all times, so only one can be out in a borrowed face. The face is always
  mirrored.
- **The Faceless:** they began with Jennet's grandson. The bargain takes your face out of the
  world's memory, so no oath can find you.
- **The empire's founding:** the church calls the war the Founding and the three the Traitor. The
  Long Sleep (three days) is the end of the war in the people's memory.
- **Their clock:** the church is drifting towards a new Red Oath (a holy war, as `troth.md`'s clock
  says). Brand wants to give the oath back, which would heal Troth and loose it on half the world;
  Jennet refuses; Aud looks to the revision and the heap of what the world rejects.
